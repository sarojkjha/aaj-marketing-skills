## Post 1 — The 85-week price test

**Source:** [Pricing Page Teardown & Price-Test Log](https://aajconsult.com/resources/pricing-page-teardown)

> Most startups that "A/B tested their pricing" didn't. They ran a test their traffic couldn't answer, stopped it the week it looked good, and shipped a guess with a p-value attached.
>
> Here's the arithmetic, with an invented but ordinary example.
>
> A $49 plan. You want to try $59. About 3,000 people a week see your pricing page, and 2% of them become paying customers. Your honest guess is that at $59, conversion drops to 1.8%.
>
> First, the break-even. $59 earns more per visitor than $49 as long as conversion stays above 1.66%. A 20% price rise can survive a drop in conversion of about 17%. It is not symmetric, and most teams never do this sum.
>
> At your guess, revenue per visitor rises about 8%. A real improvement.
>
> Now the test. To detect that 8% with the usual 95% confidence and 80% power, you need about 127,000 visitors in each arm. At 3,000 a week, that is 85 weeks.
>
> Eight weeks, which is already a long time to hold a price test clean, only lets you detect a change of about 25% in revenue per visitor. Anything smaller looks like noise.
>
> So the useful question isn't "which price won?" It's "can my traffic answer this at all?", and you ask it before you start.
>
> When the answer is no, you still have options. Ask buyers first with a price survey. Change the price for new customers and compare equal periods before and after, writing down everything else that changed. Quote the new price in the next set of sales-led deals. Or test a bigger change, because the visitors you need fall with the square of the effect.
>
> I built a free workbook that does this sum from your own numbers, plus a 21-check teardown of your pricing page. Link in the comments.

## Post 2 — Write the read-out before launch

**Source:** [Campaign Brief](https://aajconsult.com/resources/campaign-brief)

> The most useful part of a campaign brief is the part most briefs leave out: how you will judge it.
>
> After launch, everyone is invested in the answer. The team made the creative. The agency wants the renewal. You approved the budget. So the read-out finds something that went up, and the campaign "worked".
>
> The fix is boring and it works. Before any money moves, write down six things:
>
> The one metric, exactly defined.
> How you will measure it.
> What you will compare it against, and that comparison's weakness.
> The number that means it worked.
> What would make you stop or change course.
> The one date you read it.
>
> The comparison is where most read-outs quietly cheat. Compare an autumn campaign with a June-to-August baseline, and a busy season will flatter it. The same weeks last year, a region you left out, or a holdout group are all stronger, and each has its own flaw. Write the flaw down next to it.
>
> And one date, not a weekly glance. Weekly checks are for delivery and spend. Results get read once, on the day you set before launch.
>
> A campaign that missed its number and taught you something specific is a result. A campaign read out without the number written down beforehand isn't, whatever it shows.
>

## Post 3 — "If we get just 1%"

**Source:** [Bottom-Up Market Sizer](https://aajconsult.com/tools/bottom-up-market-sizer)

> "If we get just 1% of the market" is the most expensive sentence in a pitch deck. It sounds modest. It's actually a claim about how many customers you can win, disguised as a percentage.
>
> Turn it back into customers and it gets honest fast. An invented example:
>
> Your serviceable market is $15M. Your average contract is $25,000. That's 600 accounts you could realistically sell to.
>
> "We'll take 10%" means 60 new customers.
>
> Now look at who is selling. The founder closes about one and a half deals a month on a good run. That's 18 a year, or $450,000.
>
> So your obtainable market isn't 10%. It's 3%, and it's set by capacity, not ambition. To reach 10% you need roughly three times the selling capacity, and that is a hiring plan, not a market-sizing assumption.
>
> This is the step top-down sizing skips. Start from accounts you can count, with a source for each count. Apply how many actually fit and how many you can reach. Then set the obtainable number by how many wins your sales team, pipeline or self-serve funnel can actually produce.
>
> The investor who asks "how did you get that number?" is asking for exactly this chain.
>
> I built a free tool that does it segment by segment and flags every number without a source. Link in the comments.

## Post 4 — One in twenty

**Source:** [Brand Foundations Playbook](https://aajconsult.com/playbooks/brand-foundations-playbook)

> Founders often tell me brand can wait until they're bigger.
>
> Professor John Dawes of the Ehrenberg-Bass Institute has a simple illustration of why that's backwards in B2B. Suppose companies change providers of things like banking, software or telecoms about every five years. Then about one in five is looking in a given year, and about one in twenty in a given quarter.
>
> That's an assumption-driven illustration, not a law. Your category's cycle may be shorter or longer. But wherever buyers purchase rarely, the direction holds: most of the people who will ever buy from you aren't buying this quarter.
>
> Your brand is what works on them in the meantime. And what they remember is whatever you've been consistent about.
>
> Which is where startups hurt themselves. A new tagline every quarter. A new colour when the designer changes. A homepage rewrite because the team got bored. You see your brand every day; your buyers see it a few times a year. The team tires of it long before the market has learned it.
>
> What a startup needs is smaller than an agency brand book, and harder:
>
> A promise your team could break, so you'd know if you did.
> Proof a buyer can check.
> Three or four personality traits, turned into writing rules.
> A few distinctive assets, used the same way every time.
> One page that holds all of it, built into the templates people actually use.
>
> I wrote up the eight steps, including naming and when a refresh is justified. Link in the comments.

**First comment:** The Brand Foundations Playbook: https://aajconsult.com/playbooks/brand-foundations-playbook

**Why fourth:** it argues against a common founder belief. The paragraph that labels Dawes' figure as an assumption-driven illustration is deliberate: it is the honest version of a statistic usually quoted as a law.

## Post 5 — Can anyone send email as you?

**Source:** [Email Authentication Checker](https://aajconsult.com/tools/email-authentication-checker)

> Two-minute check for every founder: can someone else send email that claims to come from your domain?
>
> Since February 2024, Gmail's sender rules have required every sender to set up SPF or DKIM. Anyone sending 5,000 or more messages a day to Gmail accounts needs SPF, DKIM and DMARC.
>
> The DMARC part is where people stop reading. Gmail accepts a DMARC policy of p=none, which means "send me reports, take no action." It meets the requirement. It doesn't stop anyone sending as you, because receiving servers are told not to act on failures.
>
> Full disclosure: my own domain is at p=none right now. That's deliberate. I'm reading the reports to make sure every legitimate sender passes before I move to quarantine. Moving without reading them is how a company blocks its own invoices.
>
> The other thing that breaks quietly is SPF. Every include costs DNS lookups, and the standard caps a record at ten. Add a CRM, a helpdesk and a newsletter tool, and it's easy to go over without noticing, at which point receivers can treat SPF as failing.
>
> I built a free checker that reads your live records and shows the exact line behind every finding, and the fix. It runs in your browser, so I never see the domain you check. Link in the comments.

## Post 6 — The column most battlecards leave out

**Source:** [Battlecard Builder](https://aajconsult.com/tools/battlecard-builder)

> Most competitor battlecards I review have one column: why we win.
>
> Reps stop trusting them in about a week, the first time a prospect names something the competitor genuinely does better and the card has nothing to say.
>
> The column that makes a battlecard usable is "where they win". For each one, write the rep's next move: the question that reframes it, the buyer it doesn't matter to, or the honest answer that keeps the deal alive.
>
> Three other rules I hold cards to:
>
> Every fact has a source and a date. A competitor's pricing from last spring is worse than no fact, because the rep says it with confidence. I treat anything older than 90 days as stale. That's my rule, not a benchmark, but pick one.
>
> Every objection gets a type: a real blocker, a misunderstanding, or a smokescreen. They need different answers, and a card that treats them the same gets all three wrong.
>
> No disparaging language. It reads badly in a room, and it reads worse forwarded.
>
> I built a free Battlecard Builder that checks the card against those rules before reps see it. It flags an unsourced or stale fact, a strength with no proof, a "where they win" with no next move, and a card that's missing the honest column entirely. Link in the comments.
