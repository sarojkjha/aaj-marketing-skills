# Psychology catalog, evidence strength & the manipulation boundary

Reference for the `marketing-psychology` skill. Organized by **the friction it solves**, because that's how the problem actually presents.

**Evidence key:**
- 🟢 **Robust** — replicated widely, survives scrutiny
- 🟡 **Moderate** — real effect, but size varies heavily by context
- 🔴 **Contested** — failed or shrunk under replication; test before relying on it

Effect sizes from published studies are **directional, not forecasts**. A lift measured on a consumer checkout says little about a $50K enterprise deal.

---

## Friction 1 — Attention
*Symptom: bounce within seconds, no engagement at all.*

**Von Restorff / isolation effect** 🟢 (Hedwig von Restorff, 1933)
Distinctive items in a uniform set are remembered disproportionately.
→ *Apply:* one visually distinct element per screen — a single primary CTA in a colour used nowhere else. Two "distinct" elements cancel each other out.

**Picture superiority** 🟢
Images are recalled better than text at equivalent exposure.
→ *Apply:* show the product doing the thing rather than describing it. A screenshot of the actual output beats a paragraph about the output.

**Expectancy violation / pattern interrupt** 🟡
Attention is drawn to what breaks a predicted pattern.
→ *Apply:* open with the counterintuitive claim, not the category description. "Your NRR is 101% and your base is still leaking" outperforms "Retention analytics for SaaS."
→ *Boundary:* violating expectation to earn attention is fine; violating it to mislead about what's being offered is bait-and-switch.

---

## Friction 2 — Comprehension
*Symptom: time on page, no action. Users can't say what it does.*

This is the most under-diagnosed friction. **Test it by asking five people what the product does after ten seconds on the page.** If they can't, no amount of persuasion downstream will help.

**Processing fluency** 🟢
Information that's easier to process is judged as more true, more likable, and lower risk. Fluency effects are among the most reliable in the literature.
→ *Apply:* shorter sentences, familiar words, high contrast, conventional layout. Ease of reading is itself persuasive — which is why the `copywriting` skill weights clarity.
→ *Note:* this cuts both ways. Hard-to-read terms make claims *less* believed, regardless of merit.

**Concreteness effect** 🟢
Concrete language is processed faster and recalled better than abstract language.
→ *Apply:* "cuts onboarding from 3 weeks to 4 days" over "accelerates time-to-value." The reader can picture the first.

**Cognitive load** 🟢 (working-memory capacity ≈ 4 chunks — Cowan, 2001)
The popular "7±2" figure from Miller (1956) is widely over-applied; later work puts usable capacity closer to four.
→ *Apply:* four or fewer options in any choice set. Pricing pages with six tiers convert worse than three — not because more is worse in principle, but because comparison cost exceeds the buyer's patience.

**Curse of knowledge** 🟡
Experts can't recall not knowing, so they under-explain.
→ *Apply:* have someone outside the category read the page. Anything they stumble on is jargon regardless of how standard it feels internally.

## Friction 3 — Belief
*Symptom: they understand the claim and don't believe it.*

**Social proof** 🟢 (Robert Cialdini, *Influence*, 1984)
People infer correct behaviour from others' behaviour — **strongest when the others are similar to them.**
→ *Apply:* "used by 40 Series-A B2B SaaS teams" beats "used by 10,000 businesses" for a Series-A buyer. Relevance beats volume.
→ *Boundary:* the count must be real. Fabricated customer numbers and invented testimonials are fraud, not persuasion.

**Authority** 🟢 (Cialdini)
Credentials and demonstrated expertise increase compliance.
→ *Apply:* show the reasoning, not just the conclusion — published methodology and free frameworks demonstrate expertise more credibly than claiming it. (This is the mechanism behind AAJ's own giving-away-the-playbook strategy.)

**Specificity effect** 🟡
Precise numbers are perceived as more credible than round ones, because precision implies measurement.
→ *Apply:* "cuts setup by 43%" reads as measured; "cuts setup by 50%" reads as estimated. **Only if the precise number is real** — inventing precision to seem credible is the manipulation version.

**Blemishing / damaging admission** 🟡 (Ein-Gar, Shiv & Tormala, 2012)
Disclosing a minor negative *after* positives can increase overall evaluation, apparently by increasing perceived honesty.
→ *Apply:* "We're not the cheapest, and we don't do visual identity" makes the remaining claims more believable. Works when the admission is genuine and minor; reads as a humblebrag when it's fake ("our only flaw is we care too much").

**Consistency of evidence** 🟢
Contradictions between surfaces destroy credibility disproportionately.
→ *Apply:* the same number everywhere. A site claiming "30+ projects" on one page and "80+ engagements" on another loses more trust than either number gains.

## Friction 4 — Value
*Symptom: "interesting, but not worth it / too expensive."*

**Anchoring** 🟢 (Tversky & Kahneman, 1974)
The first number seen disproportionately influences subsequent judgment. The basic effect is robust; some downstream variants are weaker than claimed.
→ *Apply:* show the highest tier or the cost of the alternative first. "A full-time CMO costs $250K" reframes a $6,500/mo retainer.
→ *Boundary:* anchoring against a real alternative is legitimate. A fake "was $999" that was never charged is deceptive pricing.

**Framing effect** 🟢 (Tversky & Kahneman, 1981)
Logically identical information produces different choices depending on presentation.
→ *Apply:* "$3.30/day" and "$99/month" are the same price. Choose the frame that matches how the buyer budgets — daily framing for consumer, annual for enterprise procurement.

**Decoy effect / asymmetric dominance** 🟡 (Huber, Payne & Puto, 1982)
Adding an option dominated by one existing option raises that option's share. Replicates in some contexts, not all.
→ *Apply:* the classic three-tier pricing middle option. Test it — the effect is less reliable than pricing-page folklore suggests.

**Loss aversion** 🟡 (Kahneman & Tversky, 1979)
Losses loom larger than equivalent gains. The direction is well supported; **the popular "2:1" ratio and its universality are contested** (see Gal & Rucker, 2018).
→ *Apply:* "you're currently losing 14% of your base" motivates more than "you could grow 14%." Use where the loss is real and quantified.

**Endowment effect** 🟡 (Thaler, 1980)
People value what they possess above what they don't.
→ *Apply:* free trials and free tools work partly through this — usage creates a sense of ownership. It's also why AAJ's free templates convert: the framework is already *yours* before any conversation.

## Friction 5 — Risk
*Symptom: long consideration, then nothing. The downside feels bigger than the upside.*

Usually the dominant friction in B2B, where the personal cost of a bad vendor choice exceeds the personal upside of a good one.

**Ambiguity aversion** 🟢 (Ellsberg, 1961)
People prefer known risks to unknown ones — they'll take a worse certain option over an ambiguous better one.
→ *Apply:* publish the price, the scope, and the timeline. Opacity reads as risk. This is the mechanism that makes transparent pricing a conversion advantage, not just a positioning one.

**Risk reversal** 🟢
Shifting downside from buyer to seller removes the blocking objection.
→ *Apply:* a credited audit, a guarantee, a short paid pilot. AAJ's $1,200 audit credited toward a larger engagement is textbook risk reversal — it converts "what if this is wasted?" into "worst case, it's a discount."

**Status quo bias** 🟢 (Samuelson & Zeckhauser, 1988)
Doing nothing is the default, and it's a real competitor.
→ *Apply:* name "do nothing" as the alternative and quantify its cost. Most positioning compares against competitors when the actual rival is the spreadsheet they're already using.

**Consensus cost (B2B-specific)** 🟡
Multi-stakeholder buying makes internal agreement the binding constraint, not persuasion of any individual.
→ *Apply:* give the champion the artifact they need to sell internally — a one-page business case, a slide, an ROI number their CFO will accept.

## Friction 6 — Inertia
*Symptom: they agree and still don't act.*

**Defaults** 🟢 (Johnson & Goldstein, 2003)
The pre-selected option is chosen at dramatically higher rates. Among the largest, most reliable effects in the literature — organ-donation rates differ by tens of percentage points on opt-in versus opt-out framing alone.
→ *Apply:* pre-select the recommended plan; default to annual if it genuinely serves most buyers.
→ *Boundary:* this is the single most abusable principle here. A default that serves the user is good design; a default that opts them into a charge they didn't ask for is a dark pattern and increasingly illegal.

**Goal gradient** 🟢 (Kivetz, Urminsky & Zheng, 2006)
Effort increases with proximity to a goal; artificial progress toward it accelerates completion.
→ *Apply:* progress bars in onboarding; start the user at step 1 of 4 complete rather than 0 of 3.

**Implementation intentions** 🟢 (Gollwitzer, 1999)
Specifying *when and where* an action will happen substantially increases follow-through — one of the better-replicated interventions in behavioural science.
→ *Apply:* "Block 20 minutes Thursday to run your first report" converts better than "get started today."

**Commitment & consistency** 🟡 (Cialdini)
Small initial commitments increase the odds of larger consistent ones.
→ *Apply:* a free tool that takes five minutes precedes a call more effectively than asking for the call. **The commitment ladder must be real** — the small step has to deliver genuine value, or it's a bait step.

**Zeigarnik effect** 🔴 (Zeigarnik, 1927)
Incomplete tasks are supposedly recalled better. **Replication is inconsistent.**
→ *Apply:* incomplete-profile prompts are worth testing, not worth assuming.

## Effects to treat with caution

Widely cited in marketing content, weak or failed under replication:

| Effect | Status |
|---|---|
| **Ego depletion** | Large-scale replications largely failed. Don't build "decision fatigue" strategy on it. |
| **Social priming** | Foundational studies failed to replicate. Avoid. |
| **Miller's 7±2** | Superseded; working memory is closer to ~4 chunks (Cowan, 2001). |
| **Loss aversion at exactly 2:1** | Direction holds; the ratio and universality are contested. |
| **Zeigarnik** | Inconsistent replication. |
| **Subliminal persuasion** | The famous popcorn study was fabricated by its author. |

Citing a discredited study to a sophisticated buyer costs more credibility than the technique could ever earn.

## The manipulation boundary

**The test:** *does this make a true thing easier to choose, or a false thing more persuasive?*

Three questions every recommendation must pass:
1. **Is the underlying fact true?** Real deadline, real inventory, real customer count.
2. **Would you be comfortable if the customer saw the design rationale?**
3. **Does it still work if they know the technique?** Good design survives disclosure. Manipulation depends on concealment.

### Legitimate vs dark, same principle

| Principle | Legitimate | Dark pattern |
|---|---|---|
| Scarcity | "3 consulting slots this quarter" (true) | "Only 2 left!" on unlimited digital goods |
| Urgency | A real enrollment deadline | A countdown that resets on refresh |
| Social proof | "40 Series-A teams use this" (real) | "12 people are viewing" (fabricated) |
| Defaults | Recommended plan pre-selected | Pre-ticked paid add-ons |
| Anchoring | Against a real alternative's cost | "Was $999" — never charged |
| Friction | Removed for the user | Added to cancellation ("roach motel") |

### Never
- **Confirmshaming** — "No thanks, I prefer losing money"
- **Fabricated social proof** — invented counts, fake testimonials, fake viewer numbers
- **Hidden costs** — fees revealed at the final step
- **Forced continuity** — trials that auto-charge without clear notice
- **Roach motel** — easy to subscribe, deliberately hard to cancel
- **Disguised ads** — ads styled as content or system messages

### Why it's a losing trade
Beyond ethics: dark patterns are enforcement targets under the **FTC Act** (and the FTC's negative-option/click-to-cancel rulemaking), the **EU Digital Services Act**, and the **California Consumer Privacy Act**. And they underperform — manufactured urgency raises immediate conversion while raising refunds, chargebacks, and churn. **Gartner** found personalized marketing generated negative experiences for a majority of surveyed customers, who were markedly more likely to regret the purchase and less likely to buy again.

A technique that lifts trial starts 8% and raises 90-day churn 15% is a loss dressed as a win. Measure the downstream metric.
